Copper Pipe for Residential Plumbing: A Cupertino Homeowner Guide

Copper has served homes for generations, and it is still a solid choice for supply lines. Here is what to know about its types, its weak points and how it compares with PEX.

By the Cupertino 24/7 Plumbing team. Published November 16, 2026. Updated November 16, 2026.

Copper pipe carries water in a large share of American homes. It resists many kinds of damage, lasts for decades and does not fail from sunlight. It also has limits. Some water chemistry attacks it, joints can fail and the metal costs more than plastic. Knowing the tradeoffs helps you decide on a repipe.

This guide covers the main types of copper, how it fails, and how it compares with PEX. For planning a full replacement, see our repiping page.

The main types of copper pipe

TypeWall thicknessCommon use
Type K.Thickest.Buried service lines and heavy duty use.
Type L.Medium.Most residential supply lines inside the home.
Type M.Thinnest.Lighter duty supply lines where code allows.
DWV copper.Thin.Drain, waste and vent in older homes.

Which type your home has depends on its age and on the original plumber. Your plumber can read the markings on the pipe to tell you.

Where copper fails

Pinhole leaks are the most common copper failure. They form when the water chemistry, the flow speed or the soil around buried pipe slowly eats through the wall. A pinhole can sit unseen behind a wall for a long time.

Joints are the other weak point. Old solder joints can crack, and fittings at dissimilar metals can corrode. If you see green or blue crust on a copper joint, water is seeping out. See leak detection to find hidden pinholes.

Copper versus PEX

Copper

  • Long track record.
  • Rigid and neat when exposed.
  • Handles heat well.
  • Higher material cost.
  • More labor to install.

PEX

  • Flexible and fast to install.
  • Fewer fittings in a run.
  • Lower material cost.
  • Not for sunlight exposure.
  • Needs correct fittings and support.

Neither is automatically better. Copper suits exposed runs and homeowners who prefer metal. PEX suits retrofits where the pipe has to snake through finished walls. Many repipes use PEX for that reason, though copper is still common.

Signs your copper plumbing needs attention

  • Blue or green stains on pipes or fittings.
  • Repeated pinhole leaks in different spots.
  • Low pressure or discolored water at the tap.
  • Water stains on walls or ceilings with no known cause.
  • A metallic taste or smell in the water.

One leak does not always mean the whole house needs a repipe. Repeated leaks in different places do. Ask for an inspection and a clear price before work starts.

Repair or repipe

A single pinhole can be cut out and repaired. If the pipe is failing along its length, patching becomes a game of chasing leaks. A repipe replaces the supply lines in one planned project and ends the pattern.

Plan the work around the walls. Good plumbers open as little drywall as possible and patch the access points. Our plumbing installation page covers related remodel work.

Questions to ask a plumber about a repipe

  • Which material do you recommend for my house, and why?
  • How many walls will be opened and who patches them?
  • Is the permit included and who schedules the inspection?
  • What is the workmanship warranty, separate from the manufacturer warranty?
  • How long will the water be off each day?

A plumber who answers these plainly is a good sign. Ask for the answers in writing, and keep the quote with your permit paperwork.

Also ask how the plumber will protect your floors and belongings. A repipe is messy, and a careful crew lays down covers and cleans up each day.

Copper, PEX and other supply materials must be approved under the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

What to do if a copper pipe is leaking

Close the main valve, open the lowest faucet to drain the lines and switch off power to any wet area. Do not try to solder near wet pipe or near wood framing. Call a plumber and photograph the damage.

Copper, Cupertino water and permits

Cupertino water comes mostly from a private utility and from regional sources, and the chemistry varies by supplier. Water chemistry affects how copper ages, so ask your utility for its water quality report before choosing a material. Your plumber can also check the pipe wall condition.

A repipe is permitted work, and the City of Cupertino Building Division handles the permit and the inspection. Keep the final permit record when you sell the home. If you live in Sunnyvale, see our Sunnyvale page for local notes.
